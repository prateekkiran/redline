# What Goes Wrong: Recurring Contract Clause Types (Evidence Scan)

Research method: web search (12 searches used, hit the cap) + page reads (15 attempted; several targets — reddit.com entirely, avvo.com, justanswer.com, ftc.gov, consumeraffairs.com — returned fetch errors/403s in this environment and could not be read). Findings below are limited to sources that actually loaded. Ranked by strength/volume of evidence found, not by assumed real-world prevalence.

---

## 1. IP Assignment / Copyright "Rights-Grab" Clauses
**Apparent frequency: appears common** — this was the easiest clause type to find multiple independent, named, first-person accounts for, all within one piece of investigative journalism about the freelance publishing industry.

- Freelance journalist Holly Robertson, on signing a Mashable contract that transferred all copyright to the publisher: *"I didn't want to do it, obviously, but it was one of my first freelance assignments and at the time I was unwilling to push back in case it jeopardized the commission."*
- Freelance reporter Camille Lavoix had an article translated and republished without consent; when she complained, she was offered only "thirty or fifty [British pounds]." Verbatim account of discovering the theft: *"One day I received a message on Facebook from a Portuguese guy saying he'd read my article in a Portuguese paper. I told him I don't write in Portuguese and he said 'No, it's front page.'"*
- Writer Jack Davies described turning down an OZY.com assignment because the contract required work-for-hire status and barred him from writing about similar subjects without written permission, terms the outlet refused to negotiate. The article names Vice, Mashable, and Politico Europe as outlets that "demand total copyright transfer and waiver of moral rights."
- Source: [Columbia Journalism Review — "The clause freelance writers should fight to remove from their contracts"](https://www.cjr.org/watchdog/contract-rights-grab.php)

## 2. Non-Compete Clauses
**Apparent frequency: appears common among freelancers/creatives**, based on two independent first-person professional accounts (a comics/illustration veteran and a freelance marketing writer), both describing over-broad enforcement rather than the "high-level exec at a Fortune 500" stereotype.

- Illustrator/comics creator Colleen Doran writes that she has encountered "numerous young creators intimidated by non-compete clauses," some of whom turned down work for fear of violating vague terms, and describes a client that tried to enforce a **seven-year restriction four years after** a brief freelance engagement had ended. Quote: *"Non-competitive clauses have geographical and time limits and cannot restrict one's ability to earn a living."*
  Source: [Colleen Doran, "The None Compete Clause"](https://colleendoran.substack.com/p/the-none-compete-clause)
- Freelance writer Heidi Turner describes signing a non-compete with a marketing agency serving lawyers, which the client then interpreted expansively — blocking her from working with a Canadian agency ("they might expand into Canada") and from working directly with individual lawyers. Quote: *"[they] twist the terms to mean anything that furthers their cause, even if it hurts me,"* and on the one-year duration: *"when you're trying to build expertise and establish authority in an industry, one year is a lifetime."*
  Source: [Happy Freelancing (Heidi Turner) — "Insider tips: how to deal with non-competes"](https://happyfreelancing.substack.com/p/insider-tips-how-to-deal-with-non)

## 3. Auto-Renewal / Deliberately-Hard-to-Cancel Clauses
**Apparent frequency: appears common and large-scale** — this is the one clause type with regulatory/enforcement-level evidence (an active FTC lawsuit), not just individual anecdotes, suggesting it affects very large numbers of consumers.

- The FTC sued Fitness International (LA Fitness, Esporta Fitness, City Sports Club, Club Studio), alleging cancellation was restricted to in-person visits or mailed forms only, that in-person cancellation required specific staff available only limited hours, and mail cancellation required certified/registered mail at added cost to the consumer. The FTC estimated consumers were charged **"hundreds of millions of dollars"** in unwanted recurring fees, with **tens of thousands of complaints filed**. FTC Bureau of Consumer Protection director Christopher Mufarrige: *"The FTC's complaint describes a scenario that too many Americans have experienced — a gym membership that seems impossible to cancel."*
  Source: [The Lyon Firm — "LA Fitness Lawsuit: Difficult Membership Cancellations"](https://thelyonfirm.com/blog/la-fitness-lawsuit-membership-cancellation)

## 4. Unilateral Termination / "Termination for Convenience" Clauses
**Apparent frequency: unclear** — I could not find a documented individual complaint or news story for this one (only a contract-guide site's illustrative red-flag scenarios, not sourced to real named individuals). Flagging as weaker evidence than the categories above.

- A freelance-contract guide describes the pattern as a "massive red flag": clauses letting a client terminate "for convenience at any time, with no obligation to provide notice or pay for services rendered," and gives illustrative (not attributed to real named freelancers) scenarios of a designer losing a client overnight with no pay for work-in-progress, and a writer whose drafts go unpaid when a client invokes the clause mid-project.
  Source: [flag.red — "Termination for Convenience in Freelance Agreement: Risks & Red Flags"](https://flag.red/contract-guides/termination-for-convenience-in-freelance-agreement)
  Caveat: this source explicitly presents these as educational/illustrative examples, not case studies of real individuals — treat as weaker evidence than findings #1–3.

## 5. Fee Escalator / Rent-Escalation Clauses
**Apparent frequency: unclear** — found only one relevant firsthand professional observation, no individual complainant's own account.

- A business litigation attorney writes: *"In my practice, I routinely see escalation clauses in commercial leases and vendor agreements. Business owners are often surprised when routine operating costs or CPI increases push their payments far higher than expected."* The same piece notes index-based (CPI-tied) increases are "unpredictable and dramatic" and "less favored by tenants" compared to fixed increases.
  Source: [JDE Law (NYC) — "The Escalation Clause: Hidden Contract Increases Explained"](https://www.jdelaw.nyc/blog-the-escalation-clause/)
  Caveat: this is a lawyer's aggregate practice observation, not a specific documented dispute or a complainant's own words.

---

## 6. Automatic Lease Renewal Clauses (residential/commercial tenants)
**Apparent frequency: unclear** — evidence found was general legal-explainer content, not a specific tenant's documented complaint.

- A landlord-tenant law firm describes the general pattern: *"Many tenants, both commercial and residential, can suddenly find themselves on the hook for another whole year or more"* when an automatic-renewal notice deadline is missed, noting such clauses are "sometimes snuck in by landlords hoping that their tenants will just 'forget' that they needed to give notice."
  Source: [Gertsburg Licata — "Enforcing and Fighting an Automatic Renewal Provision in a Lease"](https://www.gertsburglicata.com/blog/automatic-lease-renewal/)
  Note: this overlaps conceptually with #3 (auto-renewal) but in the lease/rental context specifically rather than subscriptions/memberships.

## Could Not Find (searched for, no usable evidence)

- **Liability caps / limitation-of-liability clauses**: Multiple searches and page reads (Icertis, Hyperstart, Sirion, Commonpaper, Clark Meyers, uslawexplained) returned only legal-glossary/explainer content describing how these clauses work in the abstract. No documented individual complaint, forum post, or news story about someone actually burned by a liability cap was found.
- **Indemnity clauses**: Similarly, only contract-clause databases (Justia, LawInsider) and generic explainer content surfaced. No sourced complaint or dispute narrative found.
- **Reddit-based evidence generally**: reddit.com could not be fetched at all in this environment (all attempts errored), and web search did not surface indexed Reddit threads with specific complaint text for any clause type, despite several targeted `site:reddit.com` queries. This is a tooling/access limitation, not evidence that Reddit lacks such complaints.
- **Avvo and JustAnswer legal-Q&A pages** (which looked like they might contain real users' lease/auto-renewal questions) returned 403 errors and could not be read.
- **Unilateral ToS/policy-change clauses** (e.g., platform terms-of-service changes affecting creators/sellers) were not separately searched due to hitting the 12-search cap; no findings to report either way.
